Psychological and Behavioral Tinnitus Care

Treating More Than the Sound.

Tinnitus is a real auditory and neurological experience. But the impact tinnitus has on a person's life can extend far beyond the auditory system.

Tinnitus can disrupt sleep, command attention, interfere with concentration, increase stress, affect mood, change behavior, and create fear or anxiety about the future. Over time, these reactions can make tinnitus feel increasingly intrusive and difficult to escape.

That is why comprehensive tinnitus care must address both the perception of tinnitus and the way the brain and body respond to it.

Tinnitus may be the sound you hear. But treatment should address the life you live.

Not All In Your Head

Tinnitus Is Not All in Your Head.

One of the most damaging misconceptions about tinnitus is that psychological treatment means the condition is imaginary.

It does not.

Tinnitus is a genuine perception associated with activity within the auditory and nervous systems. However, our attention, emotions, stress response, sleep, thoughts, and behavior can influence how intrusive and distressing tinnitus becomes.

Psychological and behavioral tinnitus treatment is designed to help interrupt this cycle.

The goal is not to convince you that tinnitus is not there. The goal is to help change the power tinnitus has over your life.

Four Behavioral Approaches

Four Behavioral Approaches We Draw From

01

Tinnitus Retraining Therapy (TRT)

Tinnitus Retraining Therapy is a structured tinnitus management approach that combines directive counseling with sound therapy. TRT is based on the principle that tinnitus can become particularly intrusive when the brain assigns excessive importance or emotional significance to the tinnitus signal. Through education, counseling, and appropriate sound enrichment, treatment is designed to reduce the negative reaction associated with tinnitus and support habituation over time.

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate the tinnitus signal. The objective is for tinnitus to become less noticeable, less important, and less disruptive to everyday life.

02

Tinnitus Activities Treatment (TAT)

Tinnitus Activities Treatment takes a highly individualized approach by identifying the specific areas in which tinnitus is interfering with a patient's life. Treatment commonly focuses on four major areas: Thoughts and Emotions; Hearing and Communication; Sleep; and Concentration.

TAT allows treatment to focus on the areas that matter most to the individual patient rather than treating every person according to the same formula.

We do not simply treat tinnitus. We treat what tinnitus is doing to your life.

03

Modified Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, or CBT, is one of the most extensively studied behavioral approaches for reducing tinnitus related distress. CBT does not attempt to convince patients that tinnitus is not real, nor does it depend on making the tinnitus sound disappear.

Instead, treatment examines the relationship between thoughts, emotions, behaviors, and tinnitus distress. Our tinnitus focused approach modifies behavioral and cognitive strategies around the patient's specific tinnitus experience. The objective is to reduce catastrophic thinking, change unhelpful behavioral patterns, develop more effective coping strategies, and reduce the amount of attention and emotional energy tinnitus commands.

We may not always be able to immediately change the sound. But we can work on changing what happens when you hear it.

04

Acceptance and Attention Based Strategies

Trying constantly to suppress, monitor, measure, or fight tinnitus can sometimes make the sound even more central to daily life. Acceptance and attention based strategies can help patients reduce the struggle surrounding tinnitus and redirect attention toward meaningful activities, relationships, and experiences.

Acceptance does not mean giving up. It means reducing the amount of control tinnitus has over what you choose to do.

The goal is not accepting a smaller life because of tinnitus. It is building a bigger life around it.

Sleep and Tinnitus

Sleep and Tinnitus

For many patients, nighttime is one of the most difficult parts of living with tinnitus. Tinnitus may interfere with falling asleep. Poor sleep may increase fatigue, irritability, stress, and tinnitus awareness the following day.

Treatment may incorporate behavioral sleep strategies, nighttime sound enrichment, relaxation techniques, sleep hygiene recommendations, and other appropriate interventions. When more significant sleep difficulties are present, additional evaluation or specialized sleep treatment may be recommended.

Better sleep can be an important part of better tinnitus management.

Stress, Anxiety, and Tinnitus

Stress, Anxiety, and Tinnitus

Stress does not mean tinnitus is psychological. But stress and tinnitus can amplify one another.

Behavioral treatment helps patients recognize this feedback loop and develop strategies for reducing physiological arousal, redirecting attention, managing difficult thoughts, and responding differently when tinnitus becomes intrusive.

Fear with understanding. Helplessness with skills. Hypervigilance with flexibility. Avoidance with engagement. Loss of control with empowerment.

Meaningful Outcomes

Success Is More Than Making Tinnitus Quieter.

How do we know treatment is working? We look beyond tinnitus loudness.

Are you sleeping better? Are you thinking about tinnitus less often? Can you concentrate more easily? Are you less frightened when you notice it? Are you returning to restaurants, travel, work, hobbies, exercise, social activities, or quiet moments you had begun avoiding? Is tinnitus taking up less space in your day?

These are meaningful treatment outcomes.

Because ultimately, our goal is not simply to change a sound. It is to change your relationship with tinnitus, and restore your relationship with life.
